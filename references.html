References & Method

Every factual claim in this project, and where it comes from.

How to read this

Citations are attached at the level of the event. The sources listed under each of the 54 events support the claims made in that event's caption and its longer detail text. This is a deliberate choice: sentence-level footnoting of a narrative this size would bury the narrative, and most of these events are covered end-to-end by each work listed.

Contested and commonly-mistaken claims are cited individually. Where this project states something that corrects a widespread error, or where historians genuinely disagree, the specific claim is called out in a red note with its own sources. There are 9 such notes.

Sources are labelled by type so you can weigh them. Primary sources and institutional histories carry more weight than tertiary reference works. Where an encyclopedia entry is listed, treat it as a well-footnoted starting point for further reading rather than as the final authority — follow its own citations.

The causal links are interpretation, not fact. The 61 arrows in the network graph are editorial claims about how one event bore on another. They are listed in full below so they can be argued with. The events they connect are sourced; the assertion that one led to another is the atlas's own, and reasonable historians order these differently.

Every URL on this page was checked to resolve at the time of writing. If one has since rotted, the title and publisher are given so the work can be found elsewhere.

Reel I ORIGINS 1919–1939

FRAME 001 June 1919 Origins & Diplomacy

The Treaty of Versailles

Germany signs the treaty ending the First World War, accepting reparations, territorial losses, and strict limits on its military.

Article 231 assigned responsibility for the war to Germany and its allies — a clause read in Germany as a demand to confess sole guilt. The treaty required substantial reparations payments, returned Alsace-Lorraine to France, and capped the German army at 100,000 men with no air force, tanks, or submarines. It was negotiated primarily by Britain, France, the United States, and Italy, with Germany excluded from the talks and presented the terms to sign.

Note on “Article 231 assigned responsibility to Germany and its allies”

Article 231 names "Germany and her allies", not Germany alone. The reading of it as a confession of sole guilt is how it was received in Germany, not what the clause says.

FRAME 002 1929 Origins & Diplomacy

The Great Depression

A global economic collapse, triggered by the Wall Street crash, brings mass unemployment and political instability across Europe.

By 1932, German unemployment approached 6 million — close to a third of the workforce. Banks failed across Central Europe, export economies collapsed, and mainstream parties lost credibility as they failed to reverse the slide. The crisis hit Germany, still repaying war reparations, with particular force.

Note on “close to a third of the workforce”

German unemployment peaked near 6 million in early 1932, about 30% of the labour force. An earlier draft of this project said "roughly a quarter", which understates it.

FRAME 003 September 1931 China

Japan Invades Manchuria

The Japanese Army occupies Manchuria in northeast China and establishes the puppet state of Manchukuo.

The invasion followed a staged explosion on a Japanese-controlled railway near Mukden, used as pretext. The League of Nations condemned the action; Japan responded by withdrawing from the League in 1933. It marked the first major territorial conquest by a future Axis power, eight years before war broke out in Europe.

Note on “eight years before war broke out in Europe”

September 1931 to September 1939 is eight years. An earlier draft said "over a decade".

FRAME 004 January 1933 Origins & Diplomacy

Hitler Appointed Chancellor

Adolf Hitler is appointed Chancellor of Germany, and within months moves to eliminate all political opposition.

Hitler was appointed by President Hindenburg as head of a coalition government. The Reichstag Fire in February 1933 was used to justify emergency powers, and the Enabling Act of March 1933 allowed Hitler to enact laws without parliamentary approval, effectively ending German democracy within two months.

FRAME 005 1935–1936 Origins & Diplomacy

Rearmament & the Rhineland

Germany openly rebuilds its military and sends troops into the demilitarized Rhineland, breaking Versailles without consequence.

Conscription was reintroduced in 1935 and the Luftwaffe (air force) publicly unveiled — both forbidden under Versailles. In March 1936, German troops entered the Rhineland, a zone kept demilitarized by treaty. France and Britain, occupied with domestic concerns, did not intervene.

FRAME 007 March 1938 Origins & Diplomacy

The Anschluss

Germany annexes Austria. No foreign power intervenes militarily.

German troops crossed into Austria on 12 March 1938, and a rigged plebiscite the following month recorded near-unanimous support for union with Germany. The annexation violated Versailles explicitly. Britain and France registered protest but took no action, reinforcing a pattern of unopposed expansion.

Note on “a rigged plebiscite the following month”

German troops entered Austria on 12 March 1938; the plebiscite was held on 10 April 1938 — about four weeks later, not days.

FRAME 008 September 1938 Origins & Diplomacy

The Munich Agreement

Britain, France, Germany, and Italy sign an agreement allowing Germany to annex the Sudetenland region of Czechoslovakia.

Czechoslovakia itself was not party to the negotiations that decided its border. British Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain described the deal as securing "peace for our time." The Sudetenland contained Czechoslovakia's main border fortifications, which passed to Germany along with the territory.

FRAME 010 August 1939 Origins & Diplomacy

The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact

Germany and the Soviet Union sign a non-aggression pact, with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe into spheres of influence.

Signed by foreign ministers Vyacheslav Molotov and Joachim von Ribbentrop, the public pact bound both sides not to attack each other for ten years. A secret protocol, not disclosed until after the war, divided Poland, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania into German and Soviet zones. The pact freed Germany from the risk of a two-front war it had planned for.

Reel II BLITZKRIEG 1939–1941

FRAME 011 1 Sep 1939 Western & Central Europe

Germany Invades Poland

German forces cross the Polish border, combining air power and armor in a fast-moving assault later termed "blitzkrieg."

German forces crossed the Polish border in the early hours of 1 September, using coordinated air and armored attacks. Contrary to a persistent claim, the Polish air force was not destroyed on the ground: it had dispersed to camouflaged reserve airfields before the invasion and kept flying for roughly two weeks. Organized Polish resistance continued for about five weeks, with the last major unit surrendering on 6 October.

Note on “the Polish air force was not destroyed on the ground”

A persistent myth holds that the Luftwaffe destroyed the Polish air force at its bases in the first days. Polish squadrons had dispersed to camouflaged reserve airfields before 1 September and none remained at their pre-war bases that morning; they stayed in action for roughly two weeks.

FRAME 022 Apr 1941 North Africa & Mediterranean

Germany Occupies Yugoslavia & Greece

Germany invades both countries within weeks, rescuing Italy's stalled Greek campaign weeks before the invasion of the Soviet Union begins.

After a coup in Yugoslavia removed a government that had aligned with the Axis, and to rescue Italy's stalled campaign in Greece, Germany invaded both countries in April 1941. Both were occupied within weeks. Barbarossa then began about five weeks later than first scheduled — though how much of that delay the Balkans actually caused is disputed, with an unusually late spring thaw and unfinished logistics also cited.

Note on “how much of that delay the Balkans actually caused is disputed”

The traditional account has the Balkan campaign delaying Barbarossa by about five weeks. Later work questions how much is attributable to the Balkans, pointing to an unusually late spring thaw, flooded rivers and incomplete logistics. Treat the causal weight as contested.

FRAME 034 Aug 1942–Feb 1943 Pacific

The Battle of Guadalcanal

US Marines landed on Guadalcanal to secure an airfield Japan was building there, beginning six months of intense ground, naval, and air fighting for the island. Guadalcanal is often called the first Allied land victory over Japan. The first land defeat inflicted on Japanese forces was actually at Milne Bay, New Guinea, in August–September 1942, where Australian troops repelled a landing.

Honoring its Yalta commitment, the USSR invades Japanese-occupied Manchuria, removing any prospect of a Moscow-mediated peace.

Honoring its Yalta commitment, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and launched a large offensive into Japanese-occupied Manchuria, rapidly overwhelming Japanese forces there and removing any prospect of a negotiated peace mediated through Moscow, which some Japanese officials had been pursuing.

FRAME 052 9 Aug 1945 Pacific

The Atomic Bombing of Nagasaki

Three days after Hiroshima, a second atomic bomb is dropped on Nagasaki as Japanese leadership deliberates over surrender.

Three days after Hiroshima, the United States dropped a second atomic bomb on Nagasaki, killing an estimated 40,000–80,000 people by the end of the year. Japanese leadership, already weighing surrender, deliberated over the following days.

FRAME 053 15 Aug 1945 Pacific

Japan Announces Surrender

Emperor Hirohito announces Japan's acceptance of surrender terms in a radio broadcast — the first time most citizens had heard his voice.

Emperor Hirohito announced Japan's acceptance of Allied surrender terms in a radio broadcast to the Japanese public, the first time most citizens had heard his voice. Fighting largely ceased, though the formal surrender document remained to be signed.

Formal Japanese Surrender

Japanese representatives sign the Instrument of Surrender aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, formally ending the Second World War.

Japanese representatives signed the Instrument of Surrender aboard the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay, formally ending the Second World War — a conflict that, by most estimates, killed between 70 and 85 million people worldwide.

The map

Explore mode can place the events geographically. What the dots do and don't claim.

Coastlines

The basemap is Natural Earth 1:110m land (public domain), simplified for size. The coastlines are modern, and no political borders are drawn at all. Frontiers between 1939 and 1945 changed almost continuously — drawing any single year's would misrepresent every other, so the map shows physical geography only.

What a dot means

Not every event is a point on the ground. Each is tagged with what its placement actually claims, and the ones that are approximate are drawn with a dashed ring, with a hairline running back to the coordinate the dot stands for:

exact site — This is where the event happened. (22 events)

signed here — Signed, declared or announced here — its consequences played out elsewhere. (13 events)

wider region — A campaign spanning an area. The dot is a representative centre, not a location. (18 events)

not local — Not a geographic event. The dot is symbolic. (1 events)

So The Great Depression sits on Wall Street and The Tripartite Pact sits in Berlin, but neither event was confined to that spot — the first was worldwide, the second bound three governments on two continents. The map says where the pin is, not where the history was.
